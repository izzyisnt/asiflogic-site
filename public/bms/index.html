Design brief · rev A

Storage that cannot be orphaned.

Second-life LFP modules, assembled into outdoor-rated systems by an open-firmware controller. The interesting engineering is not the cells — it is what folds a stack of independent modules into one battery an inverter will trust, and what that fold does when a module stops answering.

01

Why this exists

A working battery system was bought, installed, and then orphaned when its manufacturer went under. The hardware was fine. The cloud service it phoned home to was not, and a battery management board that waits for a dead company's server is a very expensive brick. That failure is the design input here, not an anecdote.

So the premise is inverted. The pitch is not trust us to still be here in ten years. It is you will never need us: open firmware, commodity parts, documented ratings, and per-unit evidence that stays readable whoever is or is not still trading.

02

The architecture, in three layers

Each module already senses its own cells, balances them, and reports over CAN — that safety responsibility stays where the manufacturer put it, inside the module. The controller never takes it back. Its job is the middle box: fold N independent reports into the single battery an inverter expects, and be conservative about it.

The emulation layer is an existing GPL-3.0 project used unmodified, which is deliberate — it is reviewed by more people than we are, and it already speaks the protocols mainstream inverters accept.

03

The fold, and what it does when something goes wrong

These are the rules that decide whether the system is safe. Each one is a test in the codebase that must pass on the real implementation and fail on a deliberately broken version of it — because a check that cannot fail is not a check.

The string's limit is the weakest module's limit. Charge and discharge current are the minimum across every module, never an average and never the strongest. A series string carries every amp through its weakest link.
Any module in fault stops the string. Not de-rates it — stops it. A faulted module contributes zero to the minimum, so one module's problem is the whole string's problem.
A silent module is a faulted module. If a module misses its reporting window, its last known values are discarded rather than held. Stale data that looks healthy is worse than no data.
A missing module never averages away. If the controller expects twelve modules and hears eleven, the string goes to zero — it does not quietly re-normalise to a healthy eleven-module string. Those cells are still in series and now unmonitored.
Cell extremes are global, never averaged. The reported maximum is the highest cell anywhere in the string and the minimum is the lowest, because the mean hides exactly the outlier that protection exists to act on.
State of charge comes from the modules, never from voltage. LFP has a famously flat discharge curve; inferring charge from voltage is unreliable on this chemistry, and one widely-copied open-source implementation gets it wrong in a way that reads a full LFP cell as nearly empty. That specific bug is kept in our test suite as a control the design must fail.
Stated plainlyContactor fail-safe is enforced in hardware — a watchdog in the coil drive — not in firmware. Firmware supplies the heartbeat and the intent; losing the heartbeat opens the contactor regardless of what firmware believes. A firmware fail-safe fails at precisely the moment firmware is what failed.

No installer, owner, or operator ever makes a high-voltage DC connection in the field. Field-made HV terminations, under time pressure and bad light, are where the risk in this class of system actually lives. Removing them from the installation removes most of the hazard — and it is the only honest version of the phrase plug and play. A system whose instructions include "land the positive on the busbar" is not one.

It also means every connection can be measured, torqued, and proved before it ships, with the evidence attached to that unit's serial number. A field-made joint cannot be.

05

Outdoors is the default, not an option

Batteries live outside on purpose: it is the right answer for fire separation from anything occupied, and LFP tolerates the thermal range. The inverter is the component that sets the siting rules, and its manufacturer is unambiguous about them [M]:

SpecValue
Ingress protectionNEMA Type 4X
CoolingNatural convection — sealed case, no vents, the enclosure is the heatsink
Operating range−35 °C to +60 °C, 0–95 % RH
Mounting≥600 mm off the ground, well ventilated for heat radiation

The manual's own words are "install the equipment in a sheltered place to avoid direct sunlight, rain, and snow. Build a sunshade if it is needed." So a shade structure is not our embellishment — it is a manufacturer-stated installation condition, and it is specified into the product rather than left to the site. The practical consequence runs opposite to intuition: a sealed convection-cooled enclosure wants shade and free air, not a box. Enclosing it to "weatherproof" something already weatherproof only chokes its cooling.

Every cable penetration is glanded per cable. A single cord grip seals one cable of a stated diameter; run a bundle through it and it closes around the bundle's outline, leaving the gaps between cables open — a leak path that still looks terminated. Sealant is not a fix for that, and pressure equalisation is a separate membrane, not a gland.

06

One light, no buttons

The whole user interface is a black disc with a glowing rim, and a phone. A slow amber breath means running; a dim ember means idle; a deep red bite out of the ring means a fault; a cool violet shimmer means it wants commissioning. No numbers on the enclosure, no screen to break, no buttons to press on a live battery. Setup is a QR code, a phone joining the unit's own network, and a browser — no app to install, and none to be abandoned later.

Detail readings — per-cell voltages, temperatures, limits, history — are all there, on the phone, for anyone who wants them. They are simply not on the box.

07

Where this is honestly not finished

Stating gaps is the same discipline as stating specifications, so:

Module decode is not complete. The aggregation layer above is built and tested against synthetic module states. Reading the specific CAN dialect of the target modules requires bench work against a live module, and that work is not done. Nothing here claims otherwise.
Module electrical ratings are not yet transcribed. Until they come off the manufacturer's datasheet they are reported by the tooling as not checked — never as acceptable. An unknown that reads as "fine" is how rating bugs survive review.
Listing and permitting are not claimed. These systems are not UL-listed assemblies. That makes them appropriate for off-grid and non-grid-interactive applications — cabins, shops, barns, workshops, pumping, standby — and not appropriate for a permitted grid-tied residential interconnection. If someone needs the latter, they should buy a listed product, and we will say so.

How to read this page. Every load-bearing claim above is held in code as a check that must pass on the real design and fail on a deliberately broken version of it — a wrongly-rated part, a relay that cannot interrupt the current it is switching, an aggregator that quietly averages away a missing module. Twenty-seven such proofs and their controls run on every change, alongside about thirteen hundred other tests.
