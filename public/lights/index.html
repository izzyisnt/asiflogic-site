Drawing set · rev A

Two lion posts, eight orbs, one battery.

A pair of cast lion lamp posts arrived with no sockets in them. Rather than fit bulbs, each of the eight diffuser spheres gets a circuit board: a ring of individually addressable pixels for effect, a warm-white cluster for actual light. Power comes off a 20 kWh battery pack — which is the part that makes this an engineering problem rather than a shopping trip.

Power — convert once, at the pack

The pack runs at 204.8 volts. Feeding that to garden lighting would be both a parts problem — a check against the real bus voltage convicted five of six components outright — and a safety one, since a fault at that voltage sustains an arc. So the voltage is stepped down once, at the battery, by a single converter, and everything from there out is low-voltage. The fuse ahead of it is rated to break DC at that voltage; the common automotive blade fuse is not, and would open into an arc while looking like protection.

SHEET 2 / 3

The post — four spheres, one brain

Each post carries its own small computer. It drives the four spheres as four independent zones, so the everyday look can be a warm gas-lamp flicker while any sphere is free to do something else. The posts talk to each other over Wi-Fi and stay in step. A radar module — which sees people through weather that defeats the usual motion sensors — lifts the post from a dim idle to full output as you approach.

Open item — carried, not solved The post is a metal casting, which is a poor place to put two radios. Radar will not see through aluminium at all, and Wi-Fi is badly attenuated by it. Both need a non-metallic window: the radar behind a plastic aperture or riding inside the lowest sphere, and an external antenna for the controller. This is unresolved in rev A and is the reason the drawing set is not released for build.
SHEET 3 / 3

The orb disc — one per sphere

One board per sphere, carrying both channels. The rim does colour; the centre does light. The two are separately supplied on the same disc, which is the point of the layout: five volts does not travel, so it is generated at each sphere rather than sent up the post. Feeding all four rings from a single supply in the base — the obvious economy — loses a fifth of the rail before the first pixel and is kept in the codebase as a test that must fail.
